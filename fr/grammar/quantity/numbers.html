French grammar · Numbers, time & quantity

quatre-vingt-dix-neuf

In France, 99 is “four-twenty-ten-nine”. Numbers, prices, the time and words like beaucoup, tous and chaque come up in every conversation, and each has a trap or two. Here they are as people use them in shops, on the phone and when making plans.

First, the chart: the numbers that break the pattern

Up to 69, French counts much like English. The tiles below are the ones to learn by heart: the teens, the et un numbers, and the 70s to 90s, which are built on twenty.

Belgium and Switzerland say septante (70) and nonante (90), and parts of Switzerland say huitante (80). Everyone in France understands them.

Telling the time: everyday and 24-hour

In conversation people use a 12-hour clock with et quart, et demie, moins le quart. Timetables, appointments and the radio use the 24-hour clock, and many people say it in daily life too.

How much, how many: the quantity words

The middle column is the one to watch. Most quantity words take plain de, never des; a few take the noun directly; la plupart is the odd one out.

Comma and space. French writes 3,5 for 3.5 and 10 000 for 10,000. Say trois virgule cinq.

Phone numbers in pairs. 06 12 34 56 78 is read zéro six, douze, trente-quatre, cinquante-six, soixante-dix-huit.

Dates: day first. 14/11 is 14 November. Only the 1st is an ordinal: le premier mai, but le deux mai.
