French grammar · Articles

le, un, du … or nothing?

English says “I like coffee”, “I'm a teacher”, “I have no car”. French puts an article in the first, takes it out of the second, and changes it in the third. Here is how articles really work in everyday spoken French.

First, the big picture: four families

A French noun almost never stands alone. Before you say it, you choose one of these, and the choice says how you see the thing: a known one, any one, an amount of it, or none of it. The colours are the same all the way down the page.

le · la · l' · les

Definite

“The one we both know”, and also the whole kind: le café = coffee in general.

un · une · des

Indefinite

One, or several, that you can count: un café = a cup of coffee.

du · de la · de l'

Partitive

An amount of something you don't count: du café = (some) coffee.

de · d'

Bare “de”

What un, une, des, du, de la turn into after “not” and after a quantity: pas de café.
