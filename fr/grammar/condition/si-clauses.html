French grammar · Si-clauses

Si j'avais su…

“If” sentences in French follow a strict pairing of tenses: the tense after si decides the tense in the other half. Get the pair right and you can talk about plans (si tu veux, on y va), daydreams (si j'étais riche…) and regrets (si j'avais su…).

The golden rule

Right after si meaning “if”, never put the future or the conditional. It is si j'avais su, not “si j'aurais su”; si je suis là, not “si je serai là”. French speakers slip here too, and it is always corrected. (When si means “whether”, the future is fine: Je sais pas s'il viendra.)

The four forms you need, for the verbs you use most

The conditional uses the future stem with the imparfait endings (-ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient). The two past forms are avoir or être + the past participle, with the same helper as in the passé composé.

Either order. Si tu veux, on y va = On y va si tu veux. The tenses stay paired whichever half comes first.

S'il, not s'elle. Si shortens only before il and ils: s'il, s'ils, but si elle, si on.

Speech simplifies. For real plans, people often use the present in both halves: S'il pleut, on reste.

The classic trap

Same si, different tense, different meaning

The tense after si shows how likely or how real something is, and a few other uses of si look just like “if”.
