One adjective before the noun, one after, and both agreeing with it. A French adjective asks three questions English never asks: masculine or feminine, one or several, before or after? Here is how it works in everyday spoken French.

First, the chart: four forms, and what you actually hear

Every adjective has up to four written forms. In speech there are usually only one or two, because the plural s is silent and the feminine e is silent too. What the e does is wake up the consonant in front of it. The last column says whether the feminine sounds different.

Pattern Masculineil est… Feminineelle est… Masc. pluralils sont… Fem. pluralelles sont… Feminine sounds different?

Slide the chart sideways to see every column.

Listening tip. A consonant sound at the end is a strong hint that the word is feminine: petit ends in a vowel sound, petite ends in t.

Plural. You hear it from les, des, mes and the verb, not from the adjective. The one plural you do hear is -al → -aux.

Place. Most adjectives go after the noun. A short list of very common ones goes before. A few change meaning when they move.

The classic trap

Same adjective, new place, new meaning

A handful of adjectives can stand on either side of the noun. The rule of thumb: before the noun the meaning is figurative, or your opinion; after the noun it is literal, a plain fact.
