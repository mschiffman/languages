« Doucement ! » — telling someone to ease off, settle, and calm down

English leans on “calm down” for four different things. French keeps them apart: easing the pace, settling the state, cooling the emotions, and — the gentlest move — addressing the worry instead of ordering calm.

Vas-y doucement  (gently does it)

"Vas-y doucement." /dusmɑ̃/

“Take it easy / go gently.”


"Vas-y mollo." /mɔlo/

Very casual — same idea.


"Allez-y doucement."

Formal / plural.


"Doucement… doucement."

“Easy… easy now.” — soothing a nervous person or animal.


"Je vais faire doucement."

“I'll take it slow.” — about oneself.



Reste tranquille  (settle, rest)

"Reste tranquille."

“Settle down / stay put.” — to a child: behave, stop fidgeting.


"Repose-toi bien."

“Get some good rest.”


"Je vais rester tranquille."

“I'm going to have a quiet one.”



Calme-toi / Du calme  (handle with care)

"Calme-toi."

“Calm down.” ⚠️ Can backfire — exactly like the English.


"Du calme."

“Settle down / easy now.” — neutral, works for a group.


"Calmez-vous."

Formal / plural.


"Calmos. / Relax, Max !"

Slangy, playful.



Ne t'inquiète pas / Respire  (the gentler move — soothe, don't command)

"Ne t'inquiète pas. / T'inquiète."

“Don't worry.”


"Ça va aller."

“It's going to be okay.”


"Respire."

“Breathe.” — gentle, almost tender.



À retenir

Match the phrase to what you're actually slowing: vas-y doucement (pace), reste tranquille (state), du calme (emotions). And when someone's genuinely upset, skip the order to calm down — calme-toi often backfires — and go straight to the worry itself with respire or ça va aller. (More of these on the Reassurance page.)
